Help

What everything across Cloude, Compare, and Settings means and does.

The headline figure

The box at the top of the front page shows one number per condition — a weighted median across every user-selected forecaster that's earned a place in the merge for that condition (see "Collecting data" below), using each one's Corrected value where available, Raw otherwise, for the most refined forecast available for the target date. Weighted rather than a flat median: once a source has enough of its own scored accuracy history, its say in the merge scales with how accurate it's actually been here — a consistently good source pulls the figure more than a consistently mediocre one, rather than an equal vote regardless of track record. A source with too little scored history yet counts as a normal, equal vote until it's proven itself either way. The core idea is still to blend as many proven forecasters as possible rather than lean on one "best" source — the more (and better-weighted) voices in the mix, the more accurate the figure should become over time.

Front page cells

Rain, Temperature, and Wind always show — Pressure, Sunshine, Soil Temp, Dew Point, and Tide can each be switched on or off from Settings, since how useful they are varies a lot by person: Sunshine matters a great deal for gardening, drying washing, or anything solar-powered, and barely at all otherwise. Soil Temp, Dew Point, and Tide are off by default; Pressure and Sunshine are on. Pressure, Sunshine, Soil Temp, and Dew Point don't get their own FFV correction history the way Rain/Wind/Temperature/Pressure do — there's no second forecaster to meaningfully compare most of them against, so it wouldn't mean anything to score them. Tide is different again — see the Tide section below — it has its own learned correction (TFV) built from an entirely separate real-world data source.

Frost risk

When tonight's coldest forecast point is at or below about 4°C, with clear skies and light wind at that same hour — the combination that actually lets frost form, not just a low number on its own — the Temperature cell's background tints pale blue. No text, no icon, no notification, deliberately: a quiet visual cue rather than another warning to grow fatigued by.

Underperforming forecasters

If a source has been notably less accurate than its peers for over 30 days — deliberately much longer than the 14-day "Collecting data" bar, long enough to be a real pattern rather than a bad fortnight — it's quietly left out of the merged figure for that specific condition at this specific location, without being removed from your selection. A note appears in the Accuracy section on Compare when this happens; if it sits there unacknowledged for two more weeks, a reminder also appears on the front page. Dismissing either just clears the reminder — the exclusion itself stays in place for as long as the pattern holds, and clears automatically the moment the source's accuracy recovers.

Collecting data

A forecaster's Raw/Corrected figures on Compare, and its row in Accuracy, stay hidden behind a "Collecting data (x/14 days)" message until it has 14 distinct days of its own forecast-vs-actual comparisons — and it's excluded from the live headline merge until then too. Before that point, a handful of samples aren't enough to say anything meaningful about a forecaster one way or the other; showing numbers early would just be noise dressed up as data. The nine real sources clear this almost immediately (their one-off year-long backfill already covers hundreds of days); a source with no backfill needs roughly two weeks of real days passing, with the app opened along the way, before it clears the same bar.

Merged forecast (what if…)

On Compare, a checklist lets you include or exclude specific forecasters and see what the merged figure would have been with that exact selection. Unlike the live headline, this isn't gated by the 14-day wait — it uses each forecaster's full learned correction history regardless, since this is a hindsight comparison rather than a live decision. Useful for checking whether a forecaster you suspect is dragging the figure off course actually is.

Hour slider

A second, smaller slider under the grid shows the next 24 or 48 hours (set on the Settings page), always starting from right now — it doesn't move when the main Date slider does; the two are independent. Dragging it to a specific hour swaps Rain, Temperature, Wind, and Pressure for that hour's figure — a median across every real source, each corrected by its own FFV, the same approach the daily view uses. Dragging back to "Now" behaves as if the slider was never touched, showing the same figure as the daily view rather than a different hourly-only calculation that happens to look the same. Wherever you leave it (away from "Now") stays put — no countdown, so there's no rush to read it. It only resets when you touch the Date slider, or when you leave the app and come back. Sunshine doesn't have an hourly reading of its own (a daily total doesn't split into one), so while the hour slider is showing a specific hour it shows real hourly UV instead (Met Office only, since UV isn't blended across sources), as a percentage of that day's peak — a natural way to combine the two into one figure rather than a separate cell. At night it swaps to a moon phase icon instead, when a UV percentage would otherwise just read as a string of zeros.

Hourly graphs

Tapping any of the figures on the front page opens a graph of that figure across the next 24 or 48 hours. Rain, Temperature, Wind, and Pressure use the same multi-source, FFV-corrected figures as the Hour slider; Sunshine instead shows cloud cover hour by hour as a strip of sun/cloud/moon icons. Drag a finger along any line or bar graph to read a specific hour — the reading appears in a fixed bar above the graph rather than under your finger, so your hand never covers it.

Wind speed & direction

The wind figure shown is the windiest hour of the day, not a true gust and not a daily average — it sits between the two. It's a plain single number most days ("14"); if the windiest hour also has a genuinely notable gust — at least 5mph above sustained speed — it switches to "speed / gust" instead ("14 / 22"), so the two numbers don't jostle the layout on days a gust isn't worth mentioning. The Wind graph (tap the figure) shows the same rule for its own dashed red gust line and scrubber readout. Direction (shown as a rotating arrow next to the wind figure, with the compass letters underneath) comes from whichever real source has it available, in preference order — direction can't be meaningfully blended across sources the way speed's median can, since it's an angle, not a linear quantity. The arrow points downwind — the direction the wind is blowing toward, not the meteorological "coming from" convention — since that's more directly useful for knowing which way it'll push you.

Coastal caveat: on light-wind days near the coast, local sea breezes can develop that a 10km-resolution forecast model may miss, mistime, or understate — worth bearing in mind if you're relying on this for a day on the water.

Pressure

Tracked alongside Rain, Cloud, Wind, and Temperature, with the same real-data and FFV treatment where a real source supports it. Like Temperature, Pressure is corrected additively (an offset in hPa) rather than as a ratio, since it sits in a narrow band around ~1013hPa rather than scaling from a true zero.

Tide

Shown in its own card just below the weather headline, rather than inside it — a row with the next couple of high/low tide times for whichever tide location is currently selected. Tapping it opens a scrolling graph running from a day ago to three days ahead, with the height and time labelled at every peak and trough; tapping anywhere else along the curve reads off the predicted height at that exact moment, in the bar above the graph — the underlying model is a continuous prediction, not just a handful of marked points, so a moment between a high and a low is just as genuinely predicted as the extremes themselves.

Heights are shown in Chart Datum — the reference nautical charts and tide tables actually use, close to LAT (Lowest Astronomical Tide) — rather than the gauge's own raw land-survey reference, using each station's own published Chart Datum offset. A couple of stations don't have a confirmed offset yet and fall back to their raw gauge reading instead; the sheet says so plainly whenever that's the case, rather than silently mislabelling one for the other.

Unlike the rest of the app, Tide doesn't use Open-Meteo or any of the nine real weather sources — it's built entirely from the UK's Environment Agency Tide Gauge network, a free, no-key, real-time and historical water-level record from around 40 coastal stations. The prediction line is self-derived: the app fits the standard set of astronomical tidal cycles (the same underlying technique official tide tables use) to that station's own historical readings, then projects them forward. Once fitted, that model is saved and reused instantly on every later visit — including after fully closing and reopening the app — rather than being rebuilt live each time, since tides don't meaningfully change week to week; it quietly refits itself in the background roughly once a week to stay current, without ever holding up a location switch to do so. This is genuinely real, station-specific maths — but built from less history and fewer of those cycles than an official prediction uses, so expect solid timing with somewhat softer accuracy on the exact height and curve shape, especially here in the Bristol Channel, whose unusually large range and shallow mudflats distort the tide more than most coastlines.

TFV (Tidal Fudge Factor) is Tide's equivalent of FFV — a slow-moving, recency-weighted correction built from comparing the self-derived prediction against that same station's own real observed reading. Because both sides of that comparison come from the very same station, a genuine, persistent gap between them is real model error, which TFV gradually corrects; a short, sharp gap is far more likely to be an actual storm surge, which TFV is deliberately built to barely react to. Raw and Corrected are always both visible on the graph — the fainter line is Raw, the solid line is Corrected.

Tide locations are saved just like weather's saved places, but kept as their own separate list in Settings, since a favourite fishing or sailing spot won't always be the same place you check the weather for. Each one is automatically matched to its nearest real tide gauge. Adding a brand new location fetches that gauge's own recent history immediately (EA needs no key, so this happens live, on the spot) — but its TFV correction starts from nothing and only builds up real accuracy once the app has had a chance to compare its own predictions against real readings there over time. With more than one saved location, small dots appear underneath the row — tap one to switch straight to that location.

The Date slider on the front page moves Tide's predictions along with it, the same way it does for weather — rolling back or ahead shows what the tide model predicts for that date rather than always the live moment. The harmonic model is a continuous function of time rather than a set of separately fetched daily forecasts, so there's no separate past/future data source the way weather has; it's the same prediction either way, just centred on a different moment, with the date shown alongside the location name whenever it isn't "today".

Admiralty accuracy (optional): a saved tide location often sits some real distance from its nearest EA gauge — the app is otherwise using that gauge's tide as a stand-in for the actual place. A free UKHO Admiralty Discovery API key, set up once in Settings, lets a specific location learn its own real correction instead — separate time and height offsets for highs and lows — by comparing Admiralty's own published predictions for the nearest of their 607 real stations against what the self-derived model says for the same moments. Because Admiralty's API can't be called directly from a browser, this also needs a small relay in between (see admiralty-proxy-worker.js among the project's files, which deploys free on Cloudflare Workers and doesn't store or need to know anyone's key). Once a location has been checked, its corrected highs/lows appear as a separate list on its own tide sheet, underneath the graph — kept apart from the curve itself, since this correction doesn't apply evenly across the whole tidal cycle the way the plain Chart Datum conversion does, so the two can't honestly share one drawn line.

Solar

A standalone calculator, reached via its own button rather than living inside the Sunshine cell — deliberately kept separate, since it's a bigger, more specialised tool than a quick daily glance. It estimates what a solar panel system would generate, either looking back at a full real calendar year (historical) or ahead at the next 7 days (forecast).

The key difference from most solar calculators: it uses real, location-specific tilted irradiance for the exact panel angle and compass direction entered — either the 8-point dropdown or an exact heading in degrees — rather than a generic regional estimate. On top of that, it accounts for temperature derating (panels lose efficiency as they heat up, calculated from real historical temperature), a rough seasonal shading estimate (since a tree or chimney rarely shades a roof the same amount all year round), and panel degradation for an older system.

The financial estimate properly separates genuine self-consumption from what's actually imported or exported, rather than the common shortcut of treating every unit as both fully sold and fully bought. Household use can be entered either as a rough annual total with a day/night split, or — for a precise, no-assumptions match — pasted directly from half-hourly smart-meter data.

Map

A pannable view around any location — drag to look around, double-tap or the +/− buttons to zoom, and toggle Rain, Wind, Temperature, and Pressure independently. Terrain shading and coastline/river/lake outlines are always on, since they're map furniture rather than weather data. "Forecast for here" adopts wherever you've panned to as the app's main location, with the option to also bookmark it as a saved place; the triangle icon opens a list of everywhere you've saved to jump straight there, and tapping a triangle marker on the map itself does the same.

Coastline data is from Natural Earth (public domain, no attribution required). River, canal, and lake outlines are from OpenStreetMap, © OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database License — openstreetmap.org/copyright.

Target date & rollback

The Date slider — on both the front page and Compare — picks a target date, anywhere from 7 days ago to 7 days ahead, with today in the middle. Each page's slider is independent, so browsing a date on Compare won't change what the front page is showing. On the past side, roll back to see how each forecaster's prediction for that date changed as it approached, checked against what actually happened. On the future side, the app uses everything it's learned (FFV, see below) to turn today's forecasts into its own best prediction — the further ahead you look, the less real data is behind it, since real forecast data can only be as fresh as today's forecast run allows.

Compare page

The Condition selector and the full forecaster-by-forecaster table live on the Compare page, along with Merged forecast (what if…), Accuracy so far, and the advanced Backfill action — the front page stays focused on a quick, everyday glance.

Days out

On Compare, rows count down the days out from the target date — "1" is the last forecast issued before it. The date shown next to each row number is when that forecast was made.

Corrected vs Raw

Each eligible forecaster on Compare shows two columns. Raw is the forecast value itself. Corrected applies a learned adjustment (FFV, see below) to bring it closer to what usually turns out to be true. The status line below the table shows what really happened, sourced live from Open-Meteo — for a future target date, it just shows how far away the date is, since it hasn't happened yet.

FFV

Once a cell has enough history, Corrected shows a value adjusted by FFV — a figure the app builds up over time from comparing forecasts against what actually happened. It's tracked separately per postcode area, so different locations build their own history, and it weights recent samples more than old ones (roughly a 30-day memory) rather than a flat lifetime average — so a source that's genuinely drifted, for better or worse, gets picked up within about a month rather than being diluted by everything that came before. Temperature and Pressure are adjusted slightly differently from Rain, Cloud, and Wind under the hood — better suited to scales that have no true zero — but work the same way from the outside.

Saved places

Settings has one shared field for going somewhere, by postcode or a plain place name — either works. Switch looks at that place now without saving it; Save adds it to the list above without changing what's currently shown, so you can rename it (tap its name to edit) or switch to it whenever you like. Switching to a saved place reuses that area's existing FFV history rather than treating it as brand new each time, so only a genuinely new location triggers a fresh year-long backfill. The chip next to "Cloude" on the front page is a shortcut to switch between saved places without going into Settings.

Postcode or place name

Anywhere a location can be entered, a plain place name works just as well as a postcode — "York" or "TA6" both resolve to the same result. This doesn't cost any real accuracy: the app already only used a postcode's first 3 characters (a whole area, not a precise address), and the real limit on precision is the weather models' own resolution (10-25km for Met Office/ECMWF), which swallows the difference between the two either way.

Accuracy so far

On Compare, shows how far Raw and Corrected typically land from what actually happened, for whichever condition is selected — averaged across all "days out", weighted by sample count. Two numbers: the average error in real units (e.g. "±1.2mm"), and an approximate 0–100% closeness score (a simple heuristic, not a formal statistic). Corrected accuracy is scored honestly — each sample is checked against whatever FFV existed before that sample was folded in, not the final learned value applied backward, so it reflects how the correction would actually have performed in practice. A forecaster still under 14 days of history shows "Collecting data" here instead of a score — see above.

Accuracy over time

Below the Accuracy table, a small graph tracks the App's own merged accuracy (average error, for whichever condition is selected) once per calendar day — answering "is the blend actually getting better" rather than just its current snapshot. It only starts from whenever this was added, since there's no way to reconstruct what the merge's accuracy would have been on days before this was tracked; a new area needs at least two days of the app being opened before a line has anything to draw. This tracks the merged App figure only, not every individual forecaster, to keep both the storage and the graph itself simple.

Real forecast data

Met Office, ECMWF, GFS (US), ICON (Germany), GEM (Canada), Météo-France, JMA (Japan), BOM (Australia), and CMA (China)'s Rain, Cloud, Wind, Temperature, Pressure, Soil Temp, Dew Point, and Sunshine figures are all real model data (via Open-Meteo's Previous Runs API), not the demo formula the other forecasters currently use. Sunshine isn't a field that API provides directly — it's calculated from the same Direct Normal Irradiance data the API does provide, using the same DNI-above-120-W/m² rule Open-Meteo's own real Sunshine figures are built from elsewhere. UV is the one genuine gap: no real source here provides it in any form, so it still falls back to the demo formula. These nine were chosen for genuinely different geography and data-assimilation, not just to add numbers — more real sources may still be added over time, since Open-Meteo covers 15+ national weather services' models the same way.

Sunshine's real data only started being collected recently, so a forecaster's Sunshine figure on the front page shows "Collecting data" rather than a number until it has built up the same 14 days of real history every other real condition already needs before it's trusted — otherwise an early, unrefined figure would have shown up on an app whose other real conditions already have months of history behind them.

Automatic daily collection

A GitHub Action runs once a day, fetches a rolling window of real actual-weather and real-source data, and commits it to the repo — no precise location is stored in that file, just the postcode area it was collected for, plus dates and numbers. It's only ever collected for the one postcode area configured when the app was set up. If you open the app with a different postcode, that collected file simply isn't used — this app checks it matches before applying it, so one location's history can never get silently applied to another's. A different postcode still gets accurate real-source data of its own, just from its one-off backfill rather than the shared daily collection. The app replays the whole file fresh on every load to rebuild the configured area's accuracy figures, so opening the app more or less often doesn't change the result, and a single missed day self-heals on the next run.

Backfill (advanced)

On Compare, a one-off action that pulls a full year of real history for every real source straight into the current postcode area's FFV data, rather than waiting for the daily collection to build it up. Useful for filling the gap early on, or reaching further back than the rolling window normally keeps. It's a fairly large request, so it's not something to run often — and it only ever runs automatically the first time a genuinely new postcode is used.

Units

Rain, Temperature, Wind, and Pressure can each be set to metric or imperial independently on the Settings page — for example mm for Rain alongside mph for Wind. Rain converts between mm and inches, Wind between km/h and mph, Temperature between °C and °F, and Pressure between hPa and inHg — Cloud, Sunshine, and UV are the same either way. Soil Temp and Dew Point don't get their own separate choice — both follow whatever Temperature is already set to, since asking the same °C/°F question three times over would just be clutter. Figures are shown to the nearest whole number throughout, except Rain and Pressure-in-inHg (which keep decimal places, since a whole number would lose most of the useful precision) and accuracy scores (which need decimal precision to actually be useful, since good ones are small numbers close to zero).

Appearance

Settings has a row of colour swatches — Mint, Gold, Sand, Red, Blue, Teal, and Olive. Tapping one applies everywhere immediately, no reload needed, and the choice is remembered on this device. Only the app's own colours change; nothing about how the numbers are calculated is affected either way.

Backup & restore

Everything Cloude knows — FFV history, eligibility, saved places, theme, every setting, and tide data (locations, TFV, an Admiralty key and proxy URL if set, learned corrections) — lives only in this browser's storage on this one device. Settings has a "Copy backup to clipboard" button that gathers all of it into one block of text, and a "Restore this backup" box to paste it back in, either on this device after clearing site data, or on a different phone entirely. Restoring replaces this device's data with whatever's pasted in, so it asks for confirmation first — worth checking it's the backup you meant to use before confirming. There's no automatic backup or cloud sync; it's a manual, on-demand copy, done whenever you choose to do it.

If weather won't load

Every request to a weather source now gives up on its own after a set time (20 seconds for most lookups, 30 for the once-off year-long backfill) rather than waiting indefinitely — a stalled connection used to be able to leave the app stuck showing "Loading weather…" with no way out. If a request is still going after about 45 seconds, a message appears explaining it's taking longer than usual, with a Retry button. The app also quietly retries on its own if your connection comes back after dropping out, so Retry is there for when that doesn't happen rather than the only way to recover.
